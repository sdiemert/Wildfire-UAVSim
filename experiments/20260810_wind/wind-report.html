Summary

Wind is not a difficulty dial over most of its range. From μ = 0 to μ = 0.75 the loss rate is indistinguishable from the no-wind baseline in all four cardinal directions — 99.5% or above, against the baseline's 100.0% — even though by μ = 0.75 the wind has discarded three quarters of every off-axis spread weight and nearly doubled the front speed.

Above μ = 0.85 the loss rate collapses, and how far it collapses depends more on which way the wind blows than on how hard. At μ = 0.9 a south or west wind still loses 97% of runs while a north or east wind loses 65%. That factor of five is a property of the map rather than of the weather: the base sits a quarter of the way into the grid, so two directions have 73 rows of ground upwind of it to draw ignitions from and two have 25.

The mechanism is not that wind keeps fire away from the base. A cell burns for one update and goes out, so the fire is a travelling wave; the base takes one point of damage per step the wave stands on it and is lost at four. Wind makes the fire faster and thinner, and a thin fast fire crosses the base in two or three steps and leaves. The base survives because it was singed rather than besieged.

1. Objective and result

The no-UAV baseline established that this configuration is unwinnable: 5 000 runs, 5 000 losses, a 95% Wilson interval of 99.92 – 100.00. This experiment asks what the wind does to that, and how much of the answer is the wind rather than the map.

The question matters for two reasons. The first is practical: wind is one of the few settings that could soften a scenario which is otherwise saturated at 100%, and a saturated baseline cannot discriminate between UAV policies at the weak end. The second is that the earlier calibration experiment recorded, as a counterintuitive aside, that stronger wind made the scenario easier. That is worth either confirming with numbers or explaining properly, and it turns out to need both.

Result. Wind changes nothing the base can detect until μ > 0.75. Above 0.85 it collapses the loss rate, by a factor that depends on direction more than on strength: at μ = 0.9 the loss rate is 97.0% with a south wind and 64.6% with a north one, on the same 1 000 fires. At μ = 1 a cardinal wind leaves 0.3 – 1.6% and a diagonal wind leaves 6.8 – 20.3%.

The rest of this document sets out the arithmetic that produces that shape (§2), what was held fixed and what was swept (§3, §4), the measurements (§5), how they compare with the baseline (§6), the four checks that the surface is real rather than an artefact of a setting that failed to apply (§7), and what remains unmeasured (§8).

2. What the wind does in the model

Every result below follows from about five lines of arithmetic in sim/fire_spread.py and one property of sim/agents/fire.py that is easy to miss. They are worth setting out before the measurements rather than after them, because the naive reading of "stronger wind" predicts the opposite of what happens.

2.1 Spread

A cell that is not burning is ignited by each burning cell within a Moore radius of 3, independently, with a weight falling off as the inverse square of the distance:

w(s, s′) = d(s, s′)−2   for d ≤ 3,   0 otherwise
P(s ignites) = 1 − ∏s′ burning (1 − w(s, s′))

The wind does not add a term. It classifies each burning neighbour as lying along the wind axis or not — on_wind() counts only the three collinear downwind offsets — and moves its weight towards 1 or towards 0 by the fraction MU:

on the wind axis:  w → w + μ(1 − w)
everywhere else:   w → w(1 − μ) ← exactly a factor of (1 − μ)

The second line is the important one, and it is exact: every off-axis weight in the kernel is multiplied by (1 − μ), reaching zero only when μ is exactly 1. Meanwhile the three on-axis weights are pushed to certainty. Written out for the neighbours that matter:

Read off fire_spread.cell_weight() itself rather than worked by hand. The last row is the expected distance the downwind front advances per fire update from a single burning cell, 3w3 + 2w2(1 − w3) + (1 − w2)(1 − w3).

The wind makes the fire faster and thinner at the same time. It roughly doubles the downwind front speed — from 1.44 cells per update to a guaranteed 3.00 — while scaling everything off the axis by (1 − μ). Anyone expecting a stronger wind to be more dangerous is reading the first half of that sentence. The measurements in §5 say the second half wins.

2.2 Why thinness beats speed: the fire is a wave

A burning cell in this model burns for one fire update and then stops. It loses BURNING_RATE fuel, ignites what it can reach, and goes out. The fire is therefore a wave a few cells deep travelling across the grid, not a growing region of flame with a burning interior.

That matters because of how the home base is destroyed. The base is not destroyed by fire reaching it; it accumulates one point of damage per step on which any of its four cells is alight, and is lost at BHP = 4 of them. What kills the base is therefore dwell — how many consecutive steps the wave is standing on the footprint — and a wide slow fire dwells while a narrow fast one passes over and is gone.

The mechanism, in one sentence

Wind does not keep the fire away from the base. It makes the fire arrive sooner, cross the base in two or three steps instead of four or more, and leave. The base survives because it was singed rather than besieged.

This is visible at the extreme. At μ = 1 the fire is a single line of cells one wide, advancing three cells an update along one axis, and §7.3 shows that a line lit directly beside the base can clear the two-deep footprint in its first leap — scoring two or three damage against a requirement of four, and losing a run it geometrically should have won.

4. Method

4.1 The sweeps

Each sweep is a cartesian product of its axes run through tools/sweep.py scan, which puts every arm in its own headless.py subprocess. The subprocess matters: applying overrides mutates the global config module, so arms sharing an interpreter leak settings into one another unless every arm happens to set every key any other arm sets.

The blocks are disjoint from each other and from every other experiment in the repository: the calibration sweep used 1 000 and 500 000, and the no-UAV baseline used 1 000 000 and 2 000 000.

4.2 Independence, and pairing

Run i of an arm is seeded base_seed + i, and seed_simulation() replaces config.SYSTEM_RANDOM — the only generator the simulation draws from — with a fresh random.Random(seed) before each model is built. No state crosses between runs, and analyse.py refuses an arm that repeats a seed.

Because the two direction sweeps share one block, their arms are also paired: run 37 is the same fire in all 73 of them, so a difference between two arms is a difference between the winds and not between the fires they were shown. §7.1 verifies that rather than assuming it, and explains why it survives the extra random draw a composed wind takes.

4.3 Why the MU levels are not evenly spaced

A pilot of 100 runs per arm found the loss rate at 100% from μ = 0 to μ = 0.75 and at 2% at μ = 1.0. An evenly spaced grid would have spent nine tenths of its arms re-measuring the same flat 100%, so the ten levels put four below 0.85 — enough to establish that the flat region is genuinely flat, in every direction — and six between 0.85 and 1.0, where the response moves. Every chart in §5 therefore plots MU on an evenly spaced categorical axis; a linear one would compress the entire interesting region into the last twentieth of the width.

4.4 Interval

Every rate is reported with a 95% Wilson score interval. Many of these arms sit at or very near a boundary — 100%, or a fraction of a percent — which is exactly where the normal approximation misbehaves: at p̂ = 1 its standard error is zero and it collapses to the degenerate point [100%, 100%]. wilson() is imported from tools/sweep.py rather than reimplemented, so this experiment, the no-UAV baseline and the repository's sweep tool cannot disagree about what a 95% interval is.

4.5 Reading the arms off disk

analyse.py reads the per-arm JSON that headless.py writes, not the merged runs.csv that tools/sweep.py writes when a scan finishes. The distinction matters here: this sweep was interrupted, and an interrupted scan leaves every arm it completed on disk with no CSV at all. Those arms are perfectly good measurements and the identity of each is in its filename. An arm containing any errored run is discarded whole (§7.4).

5. Results

5.1 The four cardinal directions

40 arms, 1 000 runs each, all 40 000 runs drawn from one seed block so that run i is the same fire in every arm.

The surface has three regions, and only the third is the one anybody would have predicted.

Below μ = 0.75 the wind does not exist, as far as the base is concerned. Every one of the sixteen arms at μ ∈ {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75} lost at least 99.5% of its runs, against a no-wind baseline of 100.0%. By μ = 0.75 the wind has thrown away three quarters of every off-axis spread weight and raised the front speed from 1.44 to 2.74 cells per update — it has transformed the fire — and the loss rate has moved by a tenth of a point.

Between 0.85 and 0.99 the direction decides everything. The four curves split into two pairs that are nowhere near each other: at μ = 0.9 a south or west wind still loses 97% of runs while a north or east wind loses 65%, and at μ = 0.95 it is 51–54% against 11–13%. A factor of five, from nothing but which way the air is moving, on identical fires.

At μ = 1 everything collapses together, to between 0.3% and 1.6%. The fire is a single line one cell wide, and §7 accounts for every run that is lost, individually.

The split between the directions is the map, not the wind. The home base sits at (25, 25) on a 100×100 grid — a quarter of the way in, not in the middle. A wind that drives fire towards decreasing coordinates has 73 rows of grid upwind of the base to draw ignitions from; one driving the other way has 25. South and west are the long-approach directions, north and east the short ones, and that 73:25 ratio is the whole of the effect. It would vanish on a map with a centred base, which is worth knowing before this result is carried anywhere else.

It is worth being precise about what this does and does not say. It is not that a north wind blows the fire away from the base: at μ = 0.9 the off-axis weight is still 0.1, the fire is a broad wedge rather than a line, and fires lit well off the axis still arrive. It is that the wedge opens downwind, so the set of ignition cells that can feed it is three times larger for a south wind than a north one. The wind sets the shape; where the base sits on the map sets how much of the map that shape can catch.

5.2 The four diagonals

There is no diagonal wind direction in this simulator. Composing two perpendicular ones is how config.py says to ask for one, and it is a mixture rather than a bearing: each cell/neighbour pair blows FIRST_DIR with probability FIRST_DIR_PROB and SECOND_DIR otherwise. That distinction turns out to matter more than the bearing does.

A diagonal wind is far less protective than a cardinal one. At full strength a cardinal wind leaves a loss rate of 0.3 – 1.6%; a diagonal one leaves 6.8 – 20.3%, up to twenty times higher. A cardinal wind at μ = 1 collapses the fire to a line one cell wide. A composed one cannot: even at μ = 1 each pair of cells independently picks one of two directions, so the fire still steps two ways and stays two-dimensional.

5.3 What the wind does to the fire, as opposed to the base

A loss rate that does not move invites the reading that the setting is not connected. It is connected; it is doing a great deal to the fire, and none of it to the outcome. Mean cells burned per run under a south wind, against the loss rate of the same arms:

Between μ = 0 and 0.75 the wind removes a fifth of the burned area and changes the loss rate by one tenth of one point. By μ = 1 the fire burns 37 cells of a 10 000 cell grid — a line — and still destroys the base in one run in a hundred.

A caveat on this table

A run that is lost stops early, so burned area here mixes the extent of the fire with how long the run lasted. The comparison is sound across the top of the table, where the median step of loss moves only from 28 to 30, and progressively less so below it. The clean version of this measurement is the extent sweep described in §8, which runs with no base at all so that every run lasts the full hundred steps. It has no data yet.

6. Comparison with the baseline

The baseline lost the home base in 5 000 runs out of 5 000, a rate of 100.00% with a 95% Wilson interval of 99.92 – 100.00. Everything in §5 is a departure from that number, and this section is about how far the departures go and how much they can be trusted.

6.1 The two experiments cannot be paired

The baseline drew its fires from seeds 1 000 000 upwards; this experiment draws from 3 000 000. That is deliberate. Sharing a seed block across experiments is not pairing, it is fitting every measurement ever taken to one set of fires, and the repository's convention is that each batch gets its own block. The consequence is that the comparison here is between independent samples, and a difference of a few tenths of a point between the baseline and a μ = 0 arm is a difference between two sets of fires rather than a finding.

The honest internal comparator is therefore the μ = 0 arms: the same code, with the wind switched on and turned all the way down, on this experiment's own fires. If those do not reproduce the baseline, nothing else here can be read against it.

6.2 How far up the range the baseline survives

Ten of the forty cardinal arms have intervals that contain the baseline's 100.0% outright: all four directions at μ ≤ 0.25, and three of four at μ = 0.5. Reading a little more loosely, every arm up to μ = 0.75 sits within half a percentage point of it.

For the purposes of comparing UAV policies, switching the wind on below μ = 0.75 buys nothing. The scenario is exactly as unwinnable as the baseline, and just as saturated: it still cannot distinguish a policy that saves 0 runs in 1 000 from one that saves 1. If a later experiment needs headroom at the weak end, the wind has to be turned to 0.9 or above — and at that point the direction becomes a first-order experimental factor that has to be held fixed and reported, not left at whatever config.py happens to say.

6.3 The calibration experiment's aside, resolved

The earlier calibration batch recorded that stronger wind made the scenario easier, and attributed it to the wind concentrating the fire into a ray that misses a 2×2 base. The direction of that finding is confirmed here and the explanation is very nearly right, but it is worth sharpening on two points, because both change what it implies.

  • It is not about missing. At μ = 1 the fire does reach the base in every run where the geometry allows it — 32 of 32, §7.3. What changes is that the fire that arrives is a line, and a line does not dwell long enough to do four points of damage. Missing and passing through look the same in the loss rate and are different in every other respect, not least what a UAV would have to do about them.
  • The effect is not gradual. "Stronger wind is easier" suggests a dial. Three quarters of the dial does nothing whatsoever, and the entire effect is packed into the last fifth. Anyone tuning difficulty with MU would find it inert and then, in one step, decisive.

7. Validity

Most of this surface is flat, and a flat surface is exactly what a wind setting that silently failed to apply would also produce. Four checks separate those two readings. All four pass.

7.1 The arms see the same fires

Every arm of both direction sweeps shares one seed block, so run i should be the same fire throughout, and a difference between two arms should be a difference between the winds rather than between the fires they were shown. This holds for a reason particular to this simulator: WildFireModel.reset() draws the ignition cell at model.py:88 and only builds the FireSpread at model.py:109, so the one draw a composed wind takes from SYSTEM_RANDOM to seed its own generator lands after the cell has been chosen. The wind changes how a fire spreads without changing where it starts.

Verified rather than assumed: 1 000 seeds, each naming one ignition cell, across all 73 arms of the two sweeps.

7.2 At MU = 0 the direction cannot matter

Zero strength multiplies every weight in the kernel by one, so the four directions at μ = 0 must agree run for run — not merely in rate. Anything less would mean a direction is reaching the kernel by some path MU does not gate, and every difference reported at higher strength would be suspect.

Verified: the four arms are identical run for run, 1 000 runs each, and lose 100.00% of them (95% Wilson 99.62 – 100.00). Four identical arms is the price of being able to say the knob is wired up.

7.3 At MU = 1 the outcome is predictable run by run

At full strength the three on-axis weights are 1 and every other weight is 0, so the fire is a straight line advancing three cells an update. It should therefore be lost if and only if the ignition cell lies on the two-cell strip running through the base, on the upwind side of it. That is a prediction about which individual runs are lost, not about how many, and it is much harder to satisfy by accident than a matching rate.

The rule is stated for ignitions further than three cells from the base, and that exclusion is not bookkeeping — it is the dwell mechanism showing itself. The wave ignites everything within three cells along the axis in its first update, so a fire lit inside that radius clears the two-deep footprint in a single leap and scores two or three damage against the four required. Three runs fell inside it and one was lost. A fire lit beside the base is less dangerous than one lit twenty cells away, which is absurd as physics and exactly right as a description of this model.

7.4 An arm was discarded

The south+west arm at μ = 0.75 has 1 000 records on disk of which 256 carry a BrokenProcessPool error, because the sweep was killed while that arm was running. analyse.py discards the whole arm rather than the failed runs: keeping the survivors would report a rate over whichever runs happened to finish first, which is a biased sample dressed as a measurement. A run that raised is not a measurement, and counting it either way is how an experiment reports a bug as a result.

8. What was not run

The sweep was stopped part way. Three further sweeps were designed, are still in run.sh, and have no data. They are listed here rather than quietly dropped, because two of them bear directly on how far the results above can be carried.

SweepVariesWhat it would settle

Within the sweeps that did run, the south+west diagonal is missing at μ ≥ 0.75 — three arms never started and one was discarded (§7.4). The three complete diagonal pairs are enough to support the finding in §5.2, which is a large effect, but the fourth pair would have made it symmetric with the cardinal sweep.

None of this affects the cardinal sweep, which is complete: 40 arms, 1 000 runs each, all four directions at all ten strengths.

9. Reproducing

Everything below runs from the repository root at the commit on the title page. The sweeps are one script, because the seed blocks and the base settings have to agree between them and a list of commands in a README is a poor way to guarantee that: 

# all five sweeps. The two reported here are the first two, and take about
# an hour on eight workers; the other three were not run.
bash experiments/20260810_wind/run.sh

# arms.csv, summary.json, and the four checks of section 7
python3 experiments/20260810_wind/analyse.py

# config-used.json: all 90 settings as applied, the axes, and the commit
python3 experiments/20260810_wind/dump_config.py

# the charts and tables in section 5, regenerated from summary.json
python3 experiments/20260810_wind/figures.py all

Two things about the run are easy to get wrong and expensive to notice late. Set the run length through BATCH_SIZE, never --steps — they are aliases for one setting, and a sweep that varies both varies neither predictably. --policy random is recorded for completeness only: with NUM_AGENTS = 0 no policy is ever consulted, and naming one keeps the command identical to the baseline's.

The figures in §5 are generated rather than typed. 73 arms is more numbers than can be transcribed into HTML by hand without introducing an error that no amount of proofreading finds, so figures.py emits the tables and the SVG charts from summary.json and they are pasted in; regenerating them after a re-run is a diff rather than a re-read.

The raw per-run JSON and the merged CSVs are not in the repository. .gitignore excludes experiments/**/*.json and experiments/**/*.csv on the grounds that they run to hundreds of megabytes and are regenerable from the script above, so this report and the README beside it are the record. Every number quoted in either of them is in one of the two.
